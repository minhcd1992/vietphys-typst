when dialogical exchanges are structurally constrained, AI

The contribution of the present work becomes more
meaningful when situated against a long-standing diffi-
culty in the teaching of kinematics: the tendency to
prioritize algorithmic procedures and data substitution
over modeling, variable interpretation, and metacognitive
reflection

Research
on
active
methodologies
in science education suggests that critical thinking is
strengthened when shared argumentation, conceptual
analysis, and guided inquiry are emphasized rather than
mere repetition of procedures [3,4,43]. In translational
kinematics specifically, learning requires the construction
of mental models, the interpretation of relationships
between quantities, and abstract reasoning—competencies
that are central to the professional formation of future
physics teachers. Within this framework, the Socratic
method provides a plausible pathway for sustaining expla-
nations grounded in reasons rather than results, and its
articulation with GAI may expand opportunities for feed-
back and error reconstruction, provided that interaction
remains reflective and pedagogically supervised [6].
Within physics education, emerging studies already
report uses of ChatGPT aimed at strengthening teachers’
dialogical skills and exploring customizable Socratic
tutors, with the potential to foster active reflection and
adaptive scaffolding [44,45]. Complementary work has
shown that GAI can support conceptual understanding
and learning efficiency by offering immediate feedback,
alternative solution pathways, and opportunities for self-
correction [46]. It is also relevant that comparisons
between student-generated solutions and AI-generated
responses allow the identification of both strengths and
blind spots in each mode of reasoning, opening space for
instructional practices centered on contrast, justification,
and critical evaluation [47].
From
the
participants’
perspective,
the
observed
increase in self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation aligns
with studies reporting positive effects of GAI on engage-
ment and learning perceptions, although such effects
are contingent on task characteristics and the quality of
pedagogical guidance [48]. At the same time, the liter-
ature cautions that unguided use of chatbots may lead to
poorly structured interactions, superficial reflection, and
even reduced performance, particularly when argumenta-
tion and metacognitive control are not explicitly required
[39]. In this sense, the practical contribution of the
SOCRATIC-IA approach does not lie in “using AI” per se
but in framing its participation as a cognitive scaffold:
prompting explanations, contrasting assumptions, request-
ing evidence, checking dimensional and conceptual con-
sistency, and enforcing reasoning traceability before
accepting a response.
Nevertheless, the methodological limitations identified
call for a cautious interpretation of the findings. The
preexperimental design, fixed sequence of conditions,
and repeated use of the same problems across phases
introduce strong threats to internal validity, including
practice effects, familiarity, and potential carryover.
Additionally, the small sample size and the mixture
of
analytical
units—group-level
performance
versus
individual-level
perceptions—constrain
the
scope
of
inference. For a high editorial standard, it also remains
necessary to report the reliability of the scoring process
more explicitly, for instance, through interrater consis-
tency, and to complement statistical significance with
effect sizes and confidence intervals so that the magnitude
of change is as transparent as its statistical support.
Taken together, the study is best interpreted as situated
and preliminary evidence. It suggests that Socratic media-
tion can be strengthened through GAI when interaction is
deliberately designed to demand higher-order thinking and
when the teacher retains the role of epistemic regulator of
the dialogue. Future research should incorporate counter-
balancing or crossover designs, truly isomorphic tasks,
larger samples, and assessments of retention and transfer.
In addition, deeper qualitative analyses of the dialogues
themselves—such as question quality, justification pat-
terns, and mechanisms of self-correction—would help
disentangle more precisely which aspects of change can
be attributed to the Socratic strategy, which to GAI
mediation, and which to accumulated practice.
V. CONCLUSIONS
This study compared the traditional Socratic method
with a Socratic approach mediated by GAI to enhance
critical thinking in prospective physics teachers during the
resolution of conceptual and numerical problems in trans-
lational kinematics. The preexperimental mixed-methods
design contributes to physics education research and
related STEAM disciplines by providing empirical evi-
dence of the role of GAI as a catalyst for deep learning and
the development of higher-order cognitive skills.
The findings indicate that higher critical-thinking scores
were observed during the second, GAI-mediated Socratic
dialogue condition. Although this pattern was consistent
across the participating groups, the fixed sequential design
does not allow the observed differences to be attributed
independently to GAI mediation, because practice, accu-
mulated learning, familiarity, and carryover effects cannot
be excluded. Specifically, statistically significant within-
sample differences were observed in the quality of
