D. Qualitative analysis of student reflections across
instructional conditions
A thematic analysis suitable for small samples, follow-
ing the framework proposed by Braun and Clarke [36],
was conducted to examine the underlying experiences
reflected in the open-ended responses. In Activity 1 (A1),
students were asked, “What did you learn from the
teacher’s Socratic questions?” Analysis of the 18 res-
ponses revealed three dominant themes. The most fre-
quently reported theme was conceptual clarification and
deepening (44%), highlighting the role of guided ques-
tioning in refining understanding of kinematics concepts.
This was followed by critical thinking and metacognitive
awareness (39%), where students emphasized reflection on
assumptions, reasoning processes, and error detection. A
third theme concerned the structuring of reasoning (28%),
with respondents noting improved organization and coher-
ence in their problem-solving explanations.
In Activity 2 (A2), students responded to the question,
“In which aspects was generative AI useful or confusing?”
The thematic distribution shifted while preserving con-
tinuity with A1. Conceptual usefulness and clarity again
emerged as a dominant theme (44%), indicating that GAI
was perceived as reinforcing understanding. A substantial
proportion of responses emphasized critical use and
verification (33%), reflecting students’ awareness of the
need to evaluate, contrast, and validate AI-generated
outputs. Additional themes included structuring of reason-
ing (17%) and instrumental efficiency (17%), the latter
associated with faster exploration of solution pathways and
alternative explanations. Alongside these benefits, stu-
dents explicitly identified limitations: prompt dependence
(22%) and mathematical inaccuracies (28%), which
required continuous verification. Rather than undermining
the instructional value of GAI, these limitations appear
to have fostered more deliberate, reflective, and self-
regulated engagement with the problem-solving process.
E. Convergent mixed-methods interpretation
of learning outcomes
To integrate evidence from performance outcomes
(critical-thinking rubric), perceptions (survey data), and
reported experiences (open-ended responses), a qualitative-
quantitative convergence matrix was employed, as pre-
sented in Table IV. This integrative approach enables
systematic comparison of findings and the identification
of coherent patterns across data sources without redun-
dancy in result reporting [37,38].
Within the quantitative component, the global compari-
son between activities showed higher scores under the
GAI-mediated condition (A2), with a Wilcoxon statistic of
W ¼ 0.0000 and a p value of 0.03120. At the problem
level, statistically significant differences were identified
for Problems 1 (P1) and 4 (P4), both with p ¼ 0.03125. In
particular, P4 exhibited a marked median increase, from
6.5 in A1 to 10.0 in A2. For P2 and P3, a favorable trend
toward A2 was observed (p ¼ 0.06250), although the
conventional significance threshold of α ¼ 0.05 was not
reached. Finally, P5 showed a more moderate change, with
p ¼ 0.12500. Regarding perceptual evidence, a highly
favorable evaluation was identified. In A2, PU-GAI
reached 100% agreement. Dimensions associated with
reliability and criteria of use, such as TE-AI and RI,
maintained high levels of agreement, although with neu-
trality levels of up to 11.1% and 16.7%, respectively, and
an isolated instance of disagreement (5.6%) for RI. Taken
together, these results indicate high acceptance accompa-
nied by cautious appraisal.
The qualitative analysis revealed thematic cores that
complement the observed quantitative patterns. In A2,
conceptual usefulness and clarity emerged in 44% of
responses, critical use and verification in 33%, and
structuring of reasoning in 17%. Reported limitations
included mathematical inaccuracies (28%) and prompt
dependence (22%).
Overall, the triangulated evidence primarily indicates
convergence between performance gains and high per-
ceived usefulness, reinforced by narratives emphasizing
clarity and instructional scaffolding. In addition, partial
agreement is observed for dimensions related to trust and
resource integration, where quantitative acceptance coex-
ists with qualitative cautions highlighting the need for
verification and sensitivity to prompt formulation.
IV. DISCUSSION
This study compared two forms of Socratic mediation in
the resolution of conceptual and numerical problems in
translational kinematics within initial physics teacher
training: one guided exclusively by the instructor and
another supported by GAI. Across the full set of findings, a
consistent pattern emerges. Performance assessed through
a critical-thinking rubric and several learning-related
perceptions tended to improve when Socratic dialogue
was complemented by GAI, whereas cognitive load did not
follow a uniform trajectory and appeared to be modulated
by the additional effort required for verification and quality
control of AI-generated responses.
From a performance perspective, the observed global
improvement is consistent with recent reports positioning
AI-based tutors as supports capable of enhancing learning
in authentic educational contexts, including physics, when
they are integrated with clear pedagogical intent rather
