This design held the disciplinary content and procedural
sequence constant, allowing changes in performance to
be examined across two forms of mediation. However,
because the phases followed a fixed order, potential
practice and carryover effects are recognized as threats
to internal validity. Consequently, differences between
stages are interpreted as evidence of association compat-
ible with the introduction of GAI rather than as definitive
causal effects, consistent with classical analyses of causal
inference limitations in uncontrolled designs.
Operationally, the independent variable was the form of
Socratic mediation, contrasting teacher-guided dialogue
without GAI with teacher-guided dialogue supported
by GAI. The dependent variable was the level of critical
thinking demonstrated in the solution of one-dimensional
kinematics problems, operationalized through a single
analytic rubric designed and applied consistently across
both phases. The rubric assessed problem and principle
comprehension,
logical
reasoning
and
argumentative
structure [16,17], counterexamples [18], recognition and
correction of conceptual errors [16,19], and clarity and
organization of the written response [18]. The quanti-
tative component analyzed score variations between
stages using descriptive statistics and inferential tests
for related samples, selected according to the distributional
properties of the difference scores and accompanied by
effect size estimates, as recommended for within-subject
designs [20,21].
As a complementary measure, a perception survey was
administered at the end of each phase. In the teacher-
guided condition, the survey assessed kinematics self-
efficacy, cognitive load, intrinsic motivation, Socratic
interaction (SI), and perceived critical thinking, together
with an open-ended item designed to elicit qualitative
evidence about learning derived from the dialogue. In the
teacher-GAI condition, the survey measured kinematics
self-efficacy, cognitive load, and intrinsic motivation,
along with perceived usefulness of GAI (PU-GAI), trust
in educational AI (TE-AI), and resource integration (RI),
and included an open-ended item focused on perceived
usefulness, clarity, and potential sources of confusion
associated with AI-supported problem solving. Because
not all dimensions were common across stages, inferential
comparisons between conditions were restricted to the
shared constructs, whereas phase-specific dimensions
were reported descriptively and interpreted qualitatively,
in accordance with principles of measurement comparabil-
ity and the cautious treatment of repeated-measures
scales [20,22].
Finally, the qualitative component examined the nature
of the reasoning displayed by participants through their
open-ended responses and, when applicable, through
evidence drawn from dialogue and written problem sol-
utions. This analysis was aimed at identifying patterns of
justification, error monitoring, use of analogies or counter-
examples, and argumentative organization. The qualitative
findings were then integrated with the quantitative results
through a convergent mixed-methods strategy, in which
numerical evidence indicates the direction and magni-
tude of change, while qualitative evidence elucidates the
form and conditions under which those changes occurred,
in line with established principles for mixed-methods
integration [14].
Given the small number of participants and the use of
six collaborative groups as the unit of analysis for rubric-
based performance, the quantitative component was
treated as exploratory and complementary. Its purpose
was not to generate population-level estimates or establish
causal effects but to describe the direction, consistency,
and approximate magnitude of within-sample changes
across the two instructional conditions. Inferential tests
were therefore interpreted cautiously and were integrated
with qualitative evidence through the mixed-methods
convergence analysis.
Because the process produced a single consensus score
for each product instead of independent ratings from each
evaluator, an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) could
not be calculated. Regarding the content validity index
(CVI), we clarified that the final CVI ¼ 1.00 was obtained
only after an iterative two-round expert review. In the first
round, the analytic rubric obtained a global CVI of 0.944,
and the problem-Socratic question set obtained a global
CVI of 0.886. After revisions, the second validation round
produced a global CVI of 1.00.
B. Participants and teacher education context
The study was conducted within an initial physics
teacher training program at a public university in
Ecuador. The potential population consisted of 97 students
who had previously completed coursework in one-
dimensional kinematics. Participation was based on vol-
untary enrollment and instructional relevance, yielding a
group that was not merely selected but represented an
appropriate community for engaging in Socratic dialogue
and examining the development of critical thinking in an
authentic teacher education setting. A total of 18 students
completed all in-person sessions of the intervention,
corresponding to a participation rate of 18.6%. Participants
were enrolled between the third and eighth semesters of
their program and ranged in age from 18 to 25 years. To
support collaborative work and enable comparative analy-
sis, students were organized into six groups of three.
Group-level performance served as the unit of analysis for
critical-thinking scores, while individual contributions
were preserved through the dialogic and collaborative
writing processes.
