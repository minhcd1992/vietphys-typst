Initial physics teacher education frequently approaches kinematics problem solving as a procedural
activity, with limited engagement in conceptual reasoning, argumentation, and metacognitive regulation.
This study examines the effects of Socratic dialogue mediated by generative artificial intelligence (GAI),
compared with teacher-guided Socratic dialogue alone, on discipline-specific critical thinking (CT) in
one-dimensional kinematics. A concurrent mixed-methods, within-group preexperimental design was
implemented with 18 preservice physics teachers working in small groups across two sequential phases
using parallel kinematics problem sets. Because the instructional conditions were implemented in a fixed
order, the observed differences cannot be disentangled from possible practice, learning, and carryover
effects. In the first stage, students engaged in instructor-guided Socratic dialogue; in the second stage, the
same structure was maintained while incorporating GAI-mediated Socratic support under instructor
supervision. Critical thinking was assessed through an analytic rubric applied to written group solutions
and complemented by Likert-scale surveys and open-ended responses. Rubric scores showed consistent
improvement during the GAI-mediated phase across all groups, with no observed declines. Students also
reported higher self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation, while cognitive load exhibited heterogeneous
responses associated with the need for verification and epistemic control when interacting with GAI.
These results suggest that integrating generative AI into a teacher-mediated Socratic framework can
support critical thinking in kinematics, although the sequential preexperimental design requires cautious
interpretation.

I. INTRODUCTION
The development of critical thinking (CT) in initial
teacher training remains a persistent challenge, particu-
larly in physics, where conceptual understanding must
extend beyond the mechanical application of formulas. In
much of Latin America, instruction in one-dimensional
kinematics
has
traditionally
emphasized
algorithmic
procedures, thereby constraining the development of reflec-
tive, analytical, and metacognitive competencies required
for interpreting physical phenomena beyond numerical
computation [1,2]. This instructional pattern reveals a
substantial gap between prevailing classroom practices
and the epistemic demands of physics teacher preparation,
where reasoning, model-based interpretation, and justifica-
tion are central to disciplinary expertise.
International research has identified guided inquiry,
collaborative argumentation, and conceptual analysis as
relevant instructional approaches for supporting critical
thinking in science education. [3,4]. In physics education,
kinematics occupies a particularly demanding epistemic
space, as it requires learners to construct mental models,
interpret variable relationships, and engage in abstract
reasoning—capacities that are central to the professional
formation of physics teachers [5].
